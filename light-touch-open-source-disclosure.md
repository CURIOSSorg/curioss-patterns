# Light-Touch Open Source Disclosure

## Pattern Summary

Add a simple open source question to existing idea or invention
disclosure forms (IDFs) to understand more about the software being
created.

## Problem / Challenge

-   Many Technology Transfer Offices (TTOs) do not have a dedicated
    software disclosure form. Researchers disclose their outputs through
    a general invention or idea disclosure form, which may say little or
    nothing about software or open source.
-   As a result, open source components, licenses and release plans can
    go unnoticed until late in the process.

## Context

A university or research institution with a TTO, or similar innovation
or commercialization team, that uses a general invention, idea or IP
disclosure form rather than a dedicated software disclosure form.

An OSPO, open source champion or TTO staff member is available to follow
up with researchers who indicate in their initial disclosure that their
work includes open source software.

## Forces

-   Researchers have limited time. Long or technical forms are unlikely
    to be completed, or may be completed poorly.
-   A short form is easy to complete but captures less detail, requiring
    follow-up.
-   The TTO or OSPO may not own the institutional IP policy.
-   A single short form may need to work across very different
    disciplines and output types (e.g. biomedical projects, software,
    datasets and know-how).
-   TTO colleagues may not be fully aware of the importance or value
    proposition of open source (see [Integrating OSS into Institutional
    Software pathways](https://curioss.org/patterns/integrating-oss-into-institutional-software-pathways/)

## Solution

Start small and work within the disclosure process already in place. The
aim is to make sure open source is flagged at disclosure and explored in
conversation, rather than to create a new form.

The solution below outlines some core activities to consider.

### Add a simple open source flag to the existing form

Add one or two short questions or checkboxes to the idea, invention or
IP disclosure form, e.g. "Does this output contain software?" and "Does
it use or include any open source software?"

### Follow up with a meeting

-   Treat the form as an initial handshake: the researcher signals that
    they have something, and the TTO helps them work out what it is.
-   Arrange a short meeting with the researcher to gather the detail the
    form does not capture.

### Use an open source checklist during the meeting

Keep a short list of open source questions to work through with the
researcher, for example:

-   Which third-party or open source components does the software use,
    and under which licenses?
-   Has any of the code already been released publicly? If so, where and
    under which license?
-   Is there a plan or obligation (e.g. from a funder or collaborator)
    to release the software as open source?
-   Were AI tools or agents used to generate any part of the code?
-   Who contributed to the code, including external collaborators or
    students?

### Escalate where needed

Where the conversation shows the output is significant software, follow
up with a more detailed software disclosure or a list of dependencies
and licenses. See [Disclosing Open Source in Software Disclosure
Forms](#[Open.Source.Disclosure.Patterns.docx](https://github.com/user-attachments/files/34432291/Open.Source.Disclosure.Patterns.docx)
) for a more comprehensive approach.

### Build the case over time

Keep a simple record of what open source is disclosed. This evidence can
support a case for a dedicated software disclosure form, or for
including open source in institutional policy, in the future.

## Resulting Context

Open source is flagged at the point of disclosure without adding
significant burden for researchers or requiring changes to institutional
policy.

The follow-up meeting gives the TTO the detail it needs to identify
licensing issues early, and over time the institution builds evidence
that can support a more comprehensive approach.

## Additional Learning from ISGlobal

As a health research institution, our TTO works with both biomedical and
digital projects.

We do not have a dedicated software disclosure form. Our researchers
complete a one-page idea disclosure form that works for both biomedical
and digital projects.

Our researchers have limited time and are unlikely to complete a long
form, so we follow the short form with a meeting where we gather the
information we need.

## Additional Learning from Université du Luxembourg

We developed a lighter "IP notification" form because our invention
disclosure was too heavy for some fields.

Our IP notification is a one-pager with a general description and a few
checkboxes, e.g. whether the output is a database, may be patentable,
contains software or contains know-how.

It is a handshake rather than a full disclosure. After we discuss it
with the researcher, it may lead to one or more specific disclosures,
such as a software disclosure or a data disclosure.

For us, it does not need to be labelled as a software disclosure form: a
general invention disclosure with a question about open source can serve
the same purpose.
